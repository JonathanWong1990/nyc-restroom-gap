Verify, fix, test

New York has set a target of 2,120 public restrooms by 2035 and has 975 today. Public data cannot prove where new ones are needed. It can show that the restrooms the City already has are failing inspection more often, and it can tell Parks where to check first. So: verify where complaints point before building, fix the stock with targeted checks, and test what works with two randomised trials.

HKU MBA group projectDraft for alignment, not finalData current to June–September 2026Drafted 24 September 2026

How this differs from the current version: the ranking of neighbourhoods moves from the answer to a screen; the inspection record becomes the centre; a tested rule for where to send maintenance checks replaces the costed siting plan; the current version's evening-hours trial and pilot measures are kept; the analysis numbers were re-derived independently; the trial and pilot figures are taken from the current version. Walkthrough

The story: we set out to find where New York should build restrooms → complaints can point to places but can't prove need → the City's own inspections can prove its existing restrooms are failing more often → so we tested which ones to check first → a simple history rule works → two randomised trials (evening hours, targeted checks) would show what actually prevents problems. In short: verify, fix, test.

1 — Where is need? Complaints point, but can't prove

No dataset records "needed a toilet, found none". Closest signal: 7,926 311 "Urinating in Public" complaints, 2020–26.

  • Top 1% of addresses = 22.3% of complaints → count each address once a month. S1–2 · cleaning
  • Predict each of 197 neighbourhoods' expected complaints from riders, workers, hotels, residents; observed ÷ expected = need ratio. Beyond · negative binomial
  • Tested on unseen neighbourhoods, 200 random 70/30 splits: model 0.79 vs ridership alone 0.70 (rank correlation); model wins 96%. S4, S6–7 · correlation, train/test
  • Six areas with 95% probability of ≥1.5× expected complaints. Beyond · empirical Bayes

But complaints measure complainers. Re-ranked on phone reports only (37% of complaints), 4 of the 6 leave the top 25: Beyond · sensitivity re-run

4 — Which ones first? Inspection history predicts the next failure

  • Decision: every Jan and Jul, pick 20% of ~680 sites for an extra check, using only records entered before that date.
  • Outcome: rating at the site's next regular inspection.
  • Candidates: random, simple history rules, 4 decision trees, 2 logistic regressions. S6 · logistic S7 · trees

  • History finds ~1.8× as many failures as random; holds at 10% and 30% budgets.
  • Model vs 3-year rule: −2.3 to +3.3 pp: no convincing evidence the model beats the rule. Beyond · cluster bootstrap
  • Model's result ranges 75–88 across 9 reasonable versions; trees found no useful split.
  • "Failed last time" gets ~43% of the gain; the 3-year share gets ~95%.
  • The 3-year rule, exactly: (failures + 0.5) ÷ (rated visits + 5) over the previous three years. It was set out before any results, but the benchmark chosen on 2024 was the last-3 rule; the 3-year rule only matched the model in the test years. Treat it as the candidate to pilot, not a proven policy.
  • Not yet compared with how Parks actually targets checks today; random is a reference, not current practice.
  • 88 of 244 failures were at sites with a clean 3-year record: no history list sees those coming.
  • Independently rebuilt from raw data without the original code: same numbers, no future data leaking in.

Note: this measures better detection of failures the next inspection would find, not failures prevented.

Takeaway: inspection history concentrates later failures, and a transparent 3-year rule does as well as the model, so pilot the rule, not a model. Next: compare it with Parks' own targeting. Keep regular inspections for the third that arrive without warning.

6 — How to find out what works: two randomised tests S8–9 · A/B test

Nothing here has yet been shown to prevent anything. Two tests would change that. Both roll out in random order, so early and late sites can be compared fairly.

Test 1: longer evening hours (the "when" gap, section 2)

Hours trialDetail
StartGun Hill Playground (Williamsbridge) and Gorman Playground (East Elmhurst): park restrooms beside the largest complaint hot spots in two of the six areas, both listing the generic "8am–4pm" hours
ThenAll 51 restrooms in the 10 "close-early" areas: neighbourhoods at 1.5× or more of expected complaints whose restrooms' median posted day is under 9 hours, or where none is open to 10pm (defined in the current version)
RolloutRandom, staggered order over ten months
CostUp to $2.2M for ten months ($2.6M a year), assuming all 51 open longer for the whole period
Smallest change it can detect: complaints156%, too sparse to be useful
Smallest change it can detect: public-urination summonses Beyond · power analysis37%, with alcohol summonses as a placebo to catch changes in policing

Test 2: targeted maintenance checks (the "fix" finding, sections 3–4)

  • Among high-risk sites each half-year, randomly choose which get the extra check plus a defined repair response first.
  • Outcome: the next regularly scheduled inspection, rated the same way in both groups by inspectors not delivering the extra checks. Closures and downtime would need new, prospective logging.
  • Log what each check found and fixed, with dates and cost. Power and crew capacity still need to be set before launch.

And the 17-unit pilot: judge it on service, not units installed.

  • Its impact on complaints is hard to measure: only 20 complaints a year are filed near all 17 sites, so a before-and-after test could only detect a 73% drop (44% on summonses). Beyond · power analysis
  • Measure cost per verified open hour instead: $42.98 per unit-hour against scheduled hours, which overstates service if units are found closed. S1–2 Team research · Steph, Candy

Takeaway: the design to find out whether longer hours and targeted checks actually change conditions, not just where problems are recorded. Duration depends on the power and feasibility checks still to be done.

Limits and open questions

Every weakness of this draft in one place, starting with the questions that are still open.

Open questions: not yet resolved

  1. Did Parks change how it rates restrooms in January 2026? The jump arrives as a step that month. The same inspectors rated the rest of the park no worse, which rules out a general change in strictness, but not a restroom-only rule. The PIP rating manual or a question to Parks would settle it.
  2. Does an extra check prevent anything? The test measures failures the next regular inspection records anyway. Only the randomised design in section 6 can show prevention.
  3. How many restrooms are really operational? A preliminary match finds about 34 of the 80 long-term-closed stations still listed as operational in the June 2025 register, 1 ambiguous, and the rest already outside the 975. This shows the count needs updating; it does not give today's citywide total, which would also need openings and reopenings since then.
  4. Closed or broken? About half the 2026 rise is restrooms failed with nothing inside rated. "Found closed" is the likely reading, but it is not confirmed without the rating manual.

1. The failure jump is real, but its framing matters

  • 2024 is the lowest January–June rate in 22 years of data, so "more than doubled since 2024" flatters the change. Against all of 2025 (11.6%), 2026 is about 1.7×.
  • 2026 is back at 2012–14 levels. It reverses a decade of improvement; it is not unprecedented.
  • "Structural failures quadrupled" holds only for the 7 inspectors who rated in both years. Across all inspectors they roughly tripled.
  • A structural failure now fails the whole restroom only 20–29% of the time, against 44–61% in 2018–22. That fits either more minor defects or a lower bar for marking them; the data cannot separate the two.

2. The prediction is useful but modest

  • Ranking quality (AUC) is 0.62–0.66 in the three test periods. With a 20% budget, most recorded failures still fall outside the list.
  • The logistic model's 88 is its best case: nine reasonable specifications range from 75 to 88.
  • The three-year rule was not the benchmark chosen on 2024; the last-three rule was. The three-year rule only matched the model in the test years.
  • 43 sites fail in two or more of the three test periods and account for 91 of the 244 failures, so much of the gain is chronic sites recurring.
  • Risk levels were too low in 2026 (12% predicted, 20% observed).

3. Missing outcomes

Of 2,050 eligible test site-periods, 324 had an unrated first visit and 41 had no visit: 17.8% unknown. Unrated visits cluster at higher-risk sites. We report recorded failures only and never count a missing rating as a pass.

4. Complaints measure complainers

The neighbourhood ranking predicts complaints, not need. Four of the six high-confidence areas leave the top 25 on phone reports alone. The ratio may also partly track street homelessness or nightlife, which the model does not control for.

5. Costs are funding figures, not outturn

The $4M new-build median is the midpoint the analysis assigns to the tracker's "$3–5 million" band. On exact-dollar projects only, the medians are $3.8M for a new build and $1.37M for a reconstruction.

6. The trials are blunt

  • Complaints are too sparse to measure the hours trial (smallest detectable change 156%), so it relies on public-urination summonses (37%). Summonses partly track policing; the alcohol-summons placebo catches general patrol changes, but not a restroom-specific one.
  • The maintenance-check trial has no power calculation or crew plan yet.
  • The pilot is badly underpowered on complaints: only a 73% drop would be detectable.

7. Data dates and coverage

  • Inspections end on 28 June 2026. Any list built from them is an illustration, not a current work order.
  • Only Parks restrooms are inspected this way; libraries, transit and other operators are not covered.
  • The restroom register was last updated in June 2025.
  • Whether ratings are edited after entry cannot be tested from a single snapshot of the data.
