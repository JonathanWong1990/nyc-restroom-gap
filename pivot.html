Verify, fix, test

New York has set a target of 2,120 public restrooms by 2035; its register lists 975. Public data cannot prove where new ones are needed. It can show that the restrooms the City already has are failing inspection more often, and it can tell Parks where to check first. So: verify where complaints point before building, fix the stock with targeted checks, and test what works with two randomised trials.

Walkthrough

In one minute

  • Question: New York has promised 2,120 public restrooms by 2035; its register lists 975. Where should it act first?
  • Finding 1: public data can point to places that may need restrooms, but cannot prove it.
  • Finding 2: what the data can show is that the restrooms the City already has are failing inspection more often: one in five inspections was unacceptable in January–June 2026, the highest since 2012.
  • Finding 3: a simple rule based on each site's inspection history finds a failing restroom in about 1 in 5 checks, against 1 in 8 when checking at random.
  • Recommendation: verify, fix, test. Verify candidate sites on the ground before building. Fix the existing stock by sending checks where history points. Test what actually works with randomised trials.

1 — The problem

  • Local Law 58 (2025) sets a target of 2,120 public restrooms by 2035; the City's siting strategy is reportedly due 1 Nov 2026 (PIX11). From a source
  • The City's register lists 975 working public restrooms: 656 run by NYC Parks, the rest mainly libraries. Only 8 are open 24 hours, all highway gas stations or a snack bar. The register dates from June 2025, and about 34 restrooms Parks now lists as closed long-term are still counted in it.
  • A 17-unit modular pilot started in September 2026: $4M, one year, 7am–10pm. From a source

So the City must decide where to add over 1,100 restrooms. We set out to help answer: where is the need greatest?

2 — Where is need? Complaints can point, but not prove

No dataset records "needed a toilet and couldn't find one". The closest signal is 311, the City's complaint line: 7,926 "Urinating in Public" complaints from 2020 to 2026.

  • Cleaning. A few addresses complain repeatedly: the top 1% file 22.3% of all complaints. We count each address once a month. S1–2 · data cleaning
  • Adjusting for crowds. Busy places produce more complaints simply because more people pass through. A count model predicts each of 197 neighbourhoods' expected complaints from its subway riders, workers, hotels and residents. Neighbourhoods with far more complaints than expected are the candidates. Beyond class · negative binomial regression
  • Testing it. Trained on 70% of neighbourhoods and tested on the unseen 30%, 200 times, the model orders neighbourhoods by complaints better than subway ridership alone (rank correlation 0.79 vs 0.70, where 1 = perfect order), winning 96% of the time. S4, S7 · correlation, train/test
  • Result. Six neighbourhoods stand out, each with a 95% probability that complaints run at least 1.5× what their size and traffic predict: East Harlem (North), Astoria (East)–Woodside (North), East Elmhurst, Williamsbridge–Olinville, Brighton Beach, Midtown–Times Square. Beyond class · empirical Bayes

The limit. Complaints measure who complains, not who needed a toilet, and people who report online are not spread evenly across the city. Using only complaints made by phone (37% of the total), four of the six drop out of the top 25: Beyond class · sensitivity test

3 — When? Access runs out after dark

Timing gives a second clue: 30% of public-urination complaints are filed between 6pm and midnight, against 18% of 311 dirty-street complaints from the same callers (1.4–1.9× as high, hour by hour). That is when most restrooms have closed. S1–2 · descriptive comparison We turned every restroom's posted opening hours into hour-by-hour availability, then measured the distance from the 50 busiest subway stations to the nearest open one. Section 3 points to something section 2 could not: going by posted hours, many restrooms on the register are not available when people need them, and to the public a locked restroom is the same as no restroom. That points to a question the data can answer. Need can't be measured directly, but whether the restrooms already there are working can. So we turned to the existing restrooms.

4 — Are existing restrooms working? Failure is rising

NYC Parks keeps its own list of 736 restrooms (it calls them comfort stations). Inspectors visit them on a schedule and rate each one acceptable or unacceptable; 31,768 ratings are on file. Comparing January–June each year:

January–JuneRated unacceptable
2012–1421.2%, 17.0%, 20.0%
2022–249.4%, 9.0%, 8.5% (2024 the lowest on record)
202511.3%
202620.1%, the highest since 2012
  • It is a real change. The rise from 2024 is +11.6 percentage points (95% confidence interval +7.8 to +15.3), arriving as a step in January 2026. S3 · confidence interval
  • Ratings rose even for the same facilities. The 395 restrooms inspected every year from 2022 to 2026 went from 5.9% to 18.1%. Across all restrooms, comparing each with itself gives +11.4 points. S5 · panel data, fixed effects
  • It is not just tougher inspectors. Parks without a restroom were rated no worse (10.2% → 9.6%), and most inspectors who rated both kept their park ratings steady while failing far more restrooms. S5, S8 · difference-in-differences as a placebo
  • What kind of failure: structural problems roughly tripled while litter stayed flat. About half the rise is restrooms failed with nothing inside rated, most likely found closed or locked.

Takeaway: need is hard to observe; failure is not. One in five Parks restroom inspections was unacceptable in January–June 2026. Whether the cause is wear or closures, a restroom that fails is one the public can't rely on.

Open: we can't rule out that Parks changed its restroom rating rules in January 2026. This is the first question to put to Parks.

A citywide rise needs a citywide response, but crews are limited. So the practical question is: where should Parks send them first?

5 — Which restrooms to check first? History predicts the next failure

We framed it as a decision Parks could make twice a year: every January and July, choose 20% of its ~680 restroom sites with recent inspection history for an extra maintenance check, using only information available on that date. Success = how many of the chosen sites fail their next regular inspection. Over the three test half-years, 244 such inspections found an unacceptable restroom.

To keep the test fair, the methods learned from 2018–2023, were compared on 2024, and only then scored on 2025–mid-2026, data none of them had seen. S4, S7 · train / validation / test

How the 20% are chosen (137 checks per half-year, 411 in total)Failing restrooms foundShare of checks that found one
At random (expected)4912% (about 1 in 8)
Failure share in the last 3 inspections7919%
Failure share over the last 3 years8621% (about 1 in 5)
Logistic regression S68821%
  • History works: the 3-year rule turns about 1 in 5 checks into a found failure, against 1 in 8 at random.
  • A model adds little: logistic regression and the 3-year rule are within noise of each other (−2.3 to +3.3 percentage points of failures found), and decision trees found nothing useful. Beyond class · bootstrap confidence interval S7 · decision trees
  • Which rule: on 2024, logistic regression scored best, with the last-3-inspections rule the best simple option; on the unseen test years the 3-year rule matched the model. Because a simple rule does as well and is easier to run, we recommend piloting the 3-year rule, not declaring it proven.
  • It is useful, not precise: the best list still misses most failures: it catches 86 of the 244. And 88 of the 244 happened at sites with a clean 3-year record, which no history-based list can flag.
  • Complaints don't help here: adding nearby 311 complaints to the history barely changes the result (67 → 68 failures found on a matched sample). S6 · model comparison

Takeaway: a transparent rule (rank sites by their failure share over the past three years) points crews to failing restrooms almost twice as well as chance, with no model needed. Regular inspections must continue to catch the third that fail without warning.

This shows the rule finds failing restrooms. It does not yet show that sending crews earlier prevents failures. That needs a test.

6 — How to find out what works S8–9 · A/B test design

Test 1, targeted checks (sections 4–5)

  • Each half-year, take the high-risk sites and randomly choose which get the extra check and repair first.
  • Compare their next regular inspection, rated the same way for both groups, with the sites checked later.
  • Log what each check found and fixed, with dates and cost.

Test 2, longer evening hours (section 3)

  • Start with the park restrooms beside the two largest complaint hot spots, Gun Hill Playground (Williamsbridge) and Gorman Playground (East Elmhurst); then extend to 51 restrooms in 10 of the high-complaint neighbourhoods from section 2 whose restrooms close earliest.
  • Roll out in random order over ten months; cost up to $2.2M.
  • Complaints are too few to measure the effect, so the outcome is public-urination summonses (police tickets). A first, simplified power calculation suggests only large changes (around 37% or more) could be detected; the final calculation must reflect the staggered rollout. Beyond class · power analysis

The same thinking applies to the 17-unit pilot: judge it by the hours each unit is found open and working, not by units installed. Team research · Steph, Candy

Takeaway: two randomised tests would show whether targeted checks and longer hours actually change conditions, not just where problems are found.

7 — Recommendation: verify, fix, test

FixNYC ParksPilot extra checks, with a defined repair response, at the 20% of sites with the worst 3-year failure record (about 137 per half-year); compare with Parks' current targeting; keep regular inspections everywhere
TestParks, Mayor's Office, Department of TransportationRun both trials in random order; publish verified open hours, closures and repairs

What this does not claim: that checks or longer hours reduce failures or complaints, or any return on investment. The tests are how the City would find out.

Limits and open questions

What the analysis cannot show, and the checks behind each finding.

Open questions

  1. Did Parks change its restroom rating rules in January 2026? The failure rise arrives as a step that month. Inspectors rated parks without a restroom no worse, which rules out a general change in strictness, but not a rule change for restrooms alone. Parks' rating manual, or a direct question to Parks, would settle it.
  2. What does a failure with nothing inside rated mean? About half the 2026 rise (5.4 of 11.6 points) is of this kind. "Found closed or locked" is the likely reading but is unconfirmed.
  3. Do extra checks prevent failures? The prediction test measures failures that the next regular inspection records. Only the randomised trial in section 6 can show prevention.
  4. Does the rule beat how Parks targets crews today? It was compared with random selection, which is a reference point, not Parks' current practice. The next step is to compare it with Parks' own priority lists.

1. The failure trend

  • 2024 (8.5%) is the lowest January–June rate in 22 years of data; 2026 (20.1%) is back at 2012–14 levels. Against all of 2025 (11.6%), 2026 is about 1.7×.
  • Robustness: every month of 2026 is above the highest same month of 2022–25; removing any one inspector leaves 2026 at 18.8–21.8%, and any one borough at 17.1–22.3%; 96–100% of inspections are entered the same day.
  • Structural failures went from 5.9% to 19.2% across all inspectors. A structural failure now fails the whole restroom less often than in 2018–22 (20–29% vs 44–61%), which fits either more minor defects or a lower bar for marking them.

2. The prediction rule

  • The 3-year rule, exactly: (failures + 0.5) ÷ (rated visits + 5) over the previous three years, specified before any results were seen. On 2024, logistic regression scored best (precision 0.19), with the last-3-inspections rule the best simple option (0.18); the 3-year rule matched the model only in the test years, so it is the candidate to pilot rather than a proven policy.
  • Ranking quality (AUC) is 0.62–0.66. With a 20% budget, most failures still fall outside the list. Nine reasonable logistic versions range from 75 to 88 failures found.
  • 43 sites fail in two or more of the three test periods and account for 91 of the 244 failures: much of the gain comes from chronic sites.
  • Predicted risk levels ran low in 2026 (12% predicted, 20% observed), so they need refreshing each cycle.
  • Of 2,050 site-checks in the test period, 17.8% have no known outcome (324 unrated first visits, 41 no visit), and these lean towards riskier sites. These sites stay in the pool the 20% is chosen from; a check there can only count as a found failure if a rating exists.
  • An independent rebuild from the raw data reproduced the result exactly and found no future information in the predictions.

3. Complaints measure complainers

The neighbourhood ranking predicts complaints, not need. Four of the six high-confidence areas leave the top 25 on phone complaints alone. It may also partly reflect street homelessness or nightlife, which the model does not include.

4. Counts and costs

  • 80 of Parks' 736 comfort stations are under long-term closure (46 for repairs). About 34 of them are still listed as working in the City's register, last updated June 2025, so the 975 needs updating; we have not estimated today's true total.
  • Capital costs are funding figures, not final costs: median new build about $4M (partly a "$3–5M" band midpoint; $3.8M on exact-dollar projects), reconstruction $1.4–1.7M. They price rebuilding, not extra checks.

5. The trials

  • Complaints are too sparse to measure the hours trial (smallest detectable change 156%), so it uses summonses (37%). Both figures come from a simple Poisson calculation that ignores the staggered, clustered rollout; the real figure will differ. Summonses partly track policing; alcohol summonses act as a placebo for general patrol changes.
  • The hours-trial cost ($2.2M) assumes all 51 restrooms open longer for all ten months. The areas are the high-complaint neighbourhoods whose restrooms' median posted day is under 9 hours, or where none opens to 10pm.
  • The check trial has no power calculation or crew plan yet.
  • The 17-unit pilot can't be judged on complaints: only 20 a year are filed near all 17 sites, so only a 73% drop would be detectable (44% on summonses). Against scheduled hours it costs $42.98 per unit-hour.

6. Data dates and coverage

  • Inspections end on 28 June 2026. Only Parks restrooms are inspected this way; library and other restrooms are not covered.
  • Evening access uses straight-line distance and posted hours: walks are longer than straight lines, but actual seasonal hours may be longer than posted, so field checks are needed.
  • Whether ratings are edited after entry can't be tested from a single snapshot of the data.
