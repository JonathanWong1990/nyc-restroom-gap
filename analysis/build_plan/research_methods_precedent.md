# Research: siting methods for public facilities under unobservable demand
Prepared 2026-09-25 for PMBA6093 Final Project (NYC restroom siting, Local Law 58).

## 1. Maximal Covering Location Problem (MCLP) / set covering / p-median

**MCLP** (Church & ReVelle, 1974, *Papers in Regional Science* 32(1):101–118, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01942293, accessed 2026-09-25): choose a fixed number of new facility sites (here: some slice of the 1,145) to maximize the population/demand "covered" within a chosen distance or walk-time threshold (e.g., 5 or 10 minutes). Unlike set covering (below), it accepts that not everyone can be covered given a facility budget, and optimizes toward the best use of that budget. Over 1,500 citations; standard in ArcGIS/TransCAD network-analysis tools. Fits this case well because 1,145 is a hard cap, not open-ended, so "maximize coverage of a fixed number of sites" is exactly the decision NYC faces. Solvable via integer programming or well-known **greedy/marginal-coverage heuristics** (add whichever candidate site covers the most currently-uncovered demand, repeat) — these are simple enough to present in 12 minutes and to defend ("we added restrooms one at a time, each time picking wherever added the most newly-covered foot traffic"). Weakness: needs a defined "demand" surface (see below — this is where DOT pedestrian counts, subway ridership, jobs (LODES) and population get combined, not hand-weighted, but summed/normalized) and a defined coverage radius, both of which are themselves judgment calls, though far less arbitrary than a 4-criteria weighted score.

**Set covering problem** (Toregas et al. 1971, foundational precursor cited in the same literature): find the *minimum number* of facilities needed so every demand point is within the threshold distance — i.e., "what's the smallest network that covers everyone." Not a good fit here because NYC already knows facility count is capped at 1,145 (supply-constrained), not "minimize facilities to hit full coverage" (demand-constrained). Useful only as a sanity check ("how many restrooms would full 5-minute coverage need vs. the 1,145 we have").

**p-median problem** (classic location-science formulation; overview in Church 1976 "Theoretical and Computational Links between the p-Median, Location Set-covering and the Maximal Covering Location Problem," *Geographical Analysis* 8(1), https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1111/j.1538-4632.1976.tb00547.x, accessed 2026-09-25): place p facilities to minimize the *average* distance travelled by all demand, rather than maximizing count-within-threshold. Fits less well for restrooms — urgency-driven trips care about a hard walk-time ceiling ("can I make it in time"), not average distance, which is why toilet-siting literature converges on coverage models, not p-median. Church (1976) shows the three problems are mathematically related/nested, so this is a defensible one-line footnote rather than a separate build.

## 2. Two-step floating catchment area (2SFCA / Gaussian 2SFCA)

2SFCA (Luo & Wang 2003, foundational; widely used since) measures *supply-to-demand ratio* accessibility at each point: step 1 computes a supply/demand ratio within each facility's catchment, step 2 sums those ratios back onto each demand point within reach of multiple facilities, so someone near several restrooms scores higher than someone near just one, even if the raw distance is similar. The Gaussian variant weights nearer demand more heavily than farther, avoiding a sharp coverage cliff. Direct precedent: **"Optimization of a Layout for Public Toilets Based on Evaluation of Accessibility Through the Gaussian Two-Step Floating Catchment Area Approach,"** Kunming case study, *ISPRS International Journal of Geo-Information* 14(7):242, published 25 June 2025, https://doi.org/10.3390/ijgi14070242 (accessed 2026-09-25). That paper explicitly critiques "traditional evaluations" using raw counts/coverage as ignoring population demand, and tests 5/10/15/20/30-minute walk thresholds against a "5/10/15-minute living circle" planning concept. This is the closest peer-reviewed, toilet-specific precedent available and is directly citable. Weakness: more complex to compute and explain than MCLP; requires a "demand" and "supply capacity" figure per site (existing restrooms have no obvious capacity metric beyond stall count), and results are sensitive to the distance-decay parameter chosen, which is itself a modelling choice needing justification.

## 4. Avoiding arbitrary weights

- **Sensitivity/robustness analysis**: standard MCDA practice perturbs each weight (commonly ±10%) and recomputes rankings; reports how stable the site selection is (e.g., rank-correlation >0.92 across perturbations in EMS-siting studies used as evidence of robustness). Systematic review: "Sensitivity analysis approaches in multi-criteria decision analysis," *Omega*/ScienceDirect, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S156849462300933X (accessed 2026-09-25). This is the single most defensible answer to "why 40%?": don't defend the number, show the ranking doesn't change much even if the number moves.
- **AHP** (Saaty) derives weights from pairwise comparisons rather than picking them by hand, but is itself heavily critiqued for arbitrary/inconsistent pairwise judgments and "rank reversal" when alternatives are added/removed — see Saaty's own rebuttal "Criticisms of the Analytic Hierarchy Process: Why they often make no sense," *Mathematical and Computer Modelling*, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0895717707001033 (accessed 2026-09-25), which itself confirms the criticisms exist even while disputing them. Net: AHP does not solve the "why this number" problem, it just moves it one level up (to "why this pairwise judgment").
- **Multi-objective/Pareto framing**: instead of collapsing demand/gap/complaints/feasibility into one score, report each site's position on 2–3 separate objectives and show the Pareto-efficient frontier (sites where you can't improve one objective without worsening another). No single weight needed; the "40/30/15/15" argument disappears entirely. This is more defensible in a talk than AHP because it requires no subjective pairwise inputs at all.
- **Data-derived weights** (e.g., regression of 311 complaint density on distance/population/jobs, or PCA on the four input variables) let the data set relative importance rather than the team — more defensible than hand-picked percentages, though the 311 data is reporting-biased (stated in the prompt), so a data-derived weight partly launders that bias into the "objective" number; should be flagged as a limitation if used.

## 5. Temporal (time-of-day) accessibility

Established in the space-time accessibility literature (e.g., Neutens, Delafontaine, Scott et al., space-time GIS toolkits; Kwan's space-time accessibility measures) that ignoring opening hours **overstates** accessibility, because a facility only counts if it's open when the person needs it. Relevant academic framing: "The relationship between opening hours and accessibility of public service delivery" (https://www.academia.edu/13685257, accessed 2026-09-25) and GIS toolkit papers on person-based, time-windowed accessibility. Directly applicable: most Parks restrooms close 4pm–8am, so any coverage or 2SFCA score computed on the *full* restroom list overstates true 24-hour coverage; the team should compute coverage twice — "all listed restrooms" vs. "restrooms open right now" — and show the gap as a finding in itself, not just a caveat.

## 6. Evidence that new toilets change outcomes

Amato, H.K., Martin, D., Hoover, C.M. et al., "Somewhere to go: assessing the impact of public restroom interventions on reports of open defecation in San Francisco, California from 2014 to 2020," *BMC Public Health* 22, 2022, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12889-022-13904-4 (accessed 2026-09-25). Found SF's "Pit Stop" staffed public toilets (33 sites) associated with an average decline of ~12.47 open-defecation 311 reports/week near sites post-installation, using a 500m/6-month before-after buffer design, concentrated in the Tenderloin. This is the best available causal(ish) evidence that siting new toilets reduces the exact reporting-biased complaint signal NYC also relies on — useful both as precedent that 311 proxies respond to new supply, and as a caution that 311 complaint density is *itself* partly a function of existing supply, not just true need (i.e., circularity risk if 311 density is used as an input to site new restrooms). A 2025 follow-up, "Privy by the Bay" (PLOS ONE, https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0327795, accessed 2026-09-25), extends this with emerging-hotspot analysis of 311 waste reports 2009–2022, reinforcing the same before/after pattern.

## Recommendation for the 12-minute talk

Use **MCLP with a greedy marginal-coverage heuristic**, built on a composite *demand surface* (DOT pedestrian model + subway ridership + LODES jobs + population, combined by summation/normalization, not by a subjectively weighted score) against a **5-minute walk-time threshold restricted to hours-of-operation**, and pair it with a **sensitivity/robustness check** (vary the coverage radius and the demand-surface component weights, show the recommended sites barely change). This is more defensible than the 40/30/15/15 composite because: (a) it has a 50-year peer-reviewed pedigree (Church & ReVelle 1974) and is what ArcGIS/TransCAD already implement, so it isn't "our judgment," (b) the greedy heuristic is transparent and explainable live in a talk, (c) MCLP naturally respects the 1,145-facility cap the law already sets, which a hand-weighted score does not do without extra steps, and (d) it converts "why 40%?" into "here's the coverage-radius and demand-weight range we tested, and the top sites don't move" — a sensitivity claim instead of a values claim.

One sentence to say in the talk: **"We didn't invent a formula to rank neighborhoods — we used the same coverage-optimization method (maximal covering location, Church & ReVelle 1974) that transit and emergency-services planners use when a facility count is capped, applied to NYC's own 5-minute-walk metric, and then tested whether the results survive if our assumptions about demand are wrong."**
