# External evidence — NYC public restroom provision
Compiled 2026-09-20. All entries are from open web search/fetch; access date is the
compile date unless noted. This note covers everything NOT derivable from the project's
own datasets: cost, policy, benefit-side, method precedent, and cross-city comparison.

---
## 1. What a NYC public toilet actually costs

### Traditional Parks Dept. "comfort stations" (brick-and-mortar)
- Average cost rose from **$1.3M (2011) to $3.6M (2018/2019)** per station — nearly
  tripled in under a decade. Source: THE CITY, reported by Yoav Gonen, Apr 2019, citing
  Parks Dept and Council Member Joe Borelli.
  https://www.thecityreporter.nyc/2019/04/04/no-relief-as-pricey-park-bathrooms-put-pressure-on-taxpayers/
  (accessed 2026-09-20)
- Most expensive built-to-date example found: **Ferry Point Park West, Bronx — $4.7M**,
  completed 2018. A planned Seaside Wildlife Nature Park (Staten Island) station was
  projected near $6M. Same source.
- More recent reporting (2023) cites projects **in the $5M–$10M range**, with per-square-
  foot cost over **$5,000/sq ft**. Source: THE CITY, "Modular Portland Loo Toilets Will Be
  Tried in NYC Parks," Feb 2023. https://www.thecity.nyc/2023/02/27/portland-loo-park-bathrooms/
  (accessed 2026-09-20)
- **Two stated reasons, from named officials, not just "NYC is expensive":**
  (1) Post-Sandy flood-zone elevation requirements added structural cost (Council Member
  Borelli); (2) Parks Commissioner Mitchell Silver: "We do not tell contractors what
  number to give us — they determine what the market bears" — i.e. Parks itself does not
  claim to control the price. Both quotes in the THE CITY 2019 piece above.
- **Independent government audit confirms the pattern**: NYC Comptroller (Scott Stringer),
  *"Discomfort Stations: The Conditions and Availability of NYC Parks Bathrooms"* — found
  unacceptable conditions at 100+ park bathrooms, 38 bathrooms with safety hazards
  (17 Brooklyn, 8 Manhattan), 1,000+ without baby-changing stations, ~1,000 not ADA
  accessible. **NYC ranks 93rd of the 100 largest US cities in comfort stations per
  capita**; 10 community districts have fewer than 8 Parks bathrooms per 100,000
  residents (worst in Brooklyn/Queens). This is the single strongest "cost to NOT
  providing/maintaining" government-tier source found.
  https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/discomfort-stations-the-conditions-and-availability-of-nyc-parks-bathrooms/
  (accessed 2026-09-20; PDF itself returned 403 to automated fetch, used cached search
  summary — recommend a human open the PDF directly before citing exact page numbers)

### Cross-city cost comparison (rare, worth using)
- **NYC per-unit build cost ≈ $875,000**, vs. **Terrace, BC (Canada) ≈ $70,000**,
  **San Diego ≈ $358,000**, **Portland Loo base unit price $185,000** (before any site
  work). Source: nycpolitics101 (Substack — blog tier, not peer-reviewed, but figures are
  attributed and internally consistent with the other cost reporting above; treat as
  corroborating, not sole source).
  https://nycpolitics101.substack.com/p/why-nyc-public-toilets-cost-so-much
  (accessed 2026-09-20)
- **Named procurement-specific reasons** (same source): NYC requires special inspector
  certification for prefab units assembled outside the five boroughs (union-driven);
  stacked discretionary review — Public Design Commission, community board, Landmarks
  Preservation Commission where applicable — can add ~15 months before construction
  starts. This is a distinct, citable mechanism (not just "flood zones") and matches the
  Portland Loo manufacturer's own complaint quoted in THE CITY's 2023 piece.

### "Ur In Luck" modular pilot — the EDC/Parks program
- **Launched June 2024** under Mayor Adams: commits to **46 new restrooms + 36 renovated
  + 14 automatic public toilets (APTs) over 5 years (through 2029)**.
  https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2024/06/mayor-adams-launches-ur-luck-new-effort-make-nyc-public-restrooms-more-accessible
  (accessed 2026-09-20)
- **First delivered tranche (July 2025): 5 "Portland Loo" modular units**, one per
  borough (Irving Square Park Brooklyn, Joyce Kilmer Park Bronx, Thomas Jefferson Park
  Manhattan, Hoyt Playground Queens, Father Macris Park Staten Island).
  **Total pilot cost reported as ~$6M for the 5 units** — i.e. roughly **$1M+ per unit
  installed**, even though the Portland Loo base unit itself lists around $185,000. The
  gap is site work: new electric/water lines, foundation, ADA compliance, sidewalk
  reconstruction. **Do not quote "$185,000 restroom" and "$1M restroom" as if they
  describe the same thing — they are base-unit price vs. fully-installed price.**
  Sources: amNY, "Million dollar potties," 2025 (https://www.amny.com/news/modular-restrooms-nyc-parks-million-install/);
  Yahoo/AP syndication of same story (accessed 2026-09-20).
- **Separate, newer program**: Mayor Mamdani announced a **$4M pilot (Jan 2026)** for
  **20–30 additional modular restrooms**, RFP run through NYCEDC, explicitly aimed at
  faster/cheaper installation than the traditional comfort-station process. As of this
  compile date this is an announced RFP, not a completed build — **do not cite delivered
  unit counts for this program yet.**
  https://www.6sqft.com/mamdani-unveils-4m-pilot-program-to-expand-public-restroom-access-in-nyc/ ;
  https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/01/mayor-mamdani-announces-new-commitment-to-expand-access-to-publi0
  (accessed 2026-09-20)
- A further Sept 2026 mayoral announcement references **17 new public bathrooms** being
  brought to neighborhoods — could not fully verify whether this is the $4M/20-30-unit
  pilot's first delivery or a separate count; **flag as needing reconciliation**, not a
  clean number to present without checking the underlying press release directly.
  https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/09/mayor-mamdani-brings-17-new-public-bathrooms-to-neighborhoods-ac
  (accessed 2026-09-20, headline only)

### Operating cost (staffing/cleaning/maintenance)
- **Attended, well-resourced example**: Bryant Park comfort station costs **$271,000/year**
  to operate (2017 figure), including $27,000 toilet paper, $14,160 flowers — but this is
  funded by the Bryant Park Corporation (a BID), not typical city budget, and is widely
  cited as an outlier best-case, not a representative unit cost.
  Referenced via Urban Omnibus / Wikipedia "Bryant Park restroom" coverage (accessed 2026-09-20).
  **This figure should NOT be used as a citywide per-facility operating cost baseline —
  it is the best-funded outlier, not a median.**
- **Unattended/automatic (APT) servicing**: APTs are serviced/inspected twice daily and
  self-clean after each use; no citable per-unit annual dollar figure for APT operating
  cost was found in this pass — **gap, flag as unverified.**
- **UK comparator for a cheap alternative model**: "Community Toilet Schemes" (local
  authority pays private businesses to open their toilets to the public) cost as little
  as **£50,000/year plus £5,000 setup** citywide-scale (Havering, London borough) versus
  **£115,000/year saved by closing directly-run automated conveniences** — i.e. the
  community-scheme model was found cheaper than direct provision in this one case.
  Individual business stipends run **£750–£1,000/business/year** (Camden, Richmond).
  https://democracy.havering.gov.uk (Havering Council paper, accessed 2026-09-20);
  https://www.nelondoner.co.uk/news/30012026-nearly-half-of-london-boroughs-are-paying-businesses-to-allow-the-public-to-use-their-toilets
  (accessed 2026-09-20)
  **Relevant as a "cheapest intervention" precedent for the model's decision matrix** —
  paying existing businesses to open restrooms is a documented, costed alternative to
  new construction, at roughly 1/10th the Havering community-scheme cost of running
  city-owned automated units directly.

### Automatic Public Toilets (APT) program — Cemusa/JCDecaux — cautionary precedent
- **2006**: NYC DOT signed a 20-year franchise with **Cemusa** (Spanish street-furniture
  firm) — in exchange for exclusive ad rights on bus shelters/newsstands, Cemusa agreed
  to install and maintain **20 automatic public toilets** at no direct cost to the city,
  plus 3,500 bus shelters and 330+ newsstands.
  https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/html/infrastructure/streetfurniture.shtml (accessed 2026-09-20)
- **2008**: First APT opened, Madison Square Park.
- **2015**: Cemusa acquired by **JCDecaux**, which took over the same franchise terms
  (up to 40 APTs permitted under the amended agreement, but the 20-unit installation
  target was never met).
- **The shortfall, confirmed by multiple outlets**: a decade after the 2006 contract,
  only **5 of the 20 promised APTs were actually installed and operating**; **15 units
  sat unused in a Queens warehouse** for years (reported by Gothamist, Aug 2018, and
  corroborated by CBS News and The City Reporter, 2022, which found the situation
  essentially unchanged 2018→2022 — "15 restrooms still not in service" after a 2-year
  total stall). The city declined to specify current warehouse location when asked.
  https://gothamist.com/news/a-decade-after-their-debut-15-public-toilets-are-still-sitting-in-a-warehouse-in-queens (accessed 2026-09-20, via search cache — direct fetch blocked by site);
  https://www.thecityreporter.nyc/2022/03/29/nyc-promised-public-bathrooms-still-waiting/ (accessed 2026-09-20)
- **Why it underdelivered**: reporting attributes it to the same siting friction as the
  Parks program — finding sidewalk-width, ADA-compliant, community-board-approved
  locations for a fixed kiosk design turned out to be far harder than the contract
  assumed. This is the clearest **"procurement success, siting failure"** precedent in
  NYC's own history and directly supports the project's premise that a needs-based
  siting model (not just funding) is the binding constraint.

## 2. Local Law / policy context — the city's own numbers and targets

- **Local Law 58 of 2025** (formerly Int. 0694-2024): requires the city to establish and
  maintain a citywide network with a goal of **at least 2,120 public restrooms by 2035**,
  **at least half publicly owned**. Passed July 2025.
  https://council.nyc.gov/press/2025/07/12/2922/ (accessed 2026-09-20)
- **Local Law 92 of 2025** (Int. 0272-A): amends the citywide bathroom strategy to require
  ongoing reporting on capital projects tied to sites identified under the earlier
  **Local Law 114 of 2022** (which first mandated a public bathroom siting/strategy
  report). https://legistar.council.nyc.gov (Int 0272-2024 legislative file, accessed 2026-09-20)
- **CORRECTED 2026-09-20:** the two figures marked (*) below are **NOT in the Council's
  *Good To Go?* PDF** (full text searched; neither string appears). They come from a separate
  NYC Council dashboard, https://council.nyc.gov/data/public-restroom-analysis/, which
  publishes **no methodology**. Do not compare an independently computed coverage figure
  against the 49.42%.
- **NYC Council's own citywide assessment** ("A Citywide Assessment of Public Restrooms,"
  OID, Dec 2025) gives the baseline the 2,120-by-2035 target is measured against:
  - **1,064 public restrooms citywide**, of which **973 operational**
  - **Only 8 open 24/7**
  - (*) **1 restroom per 9,035 residents** — DASHBOARD, not the PDF; denominator undisclosed
  - (*) **49.42% of residents within a 5-minute walk** — DASHBOARD, not the PDF; method undisclosed
  - By type: Parks 822 (77%), Libraries 216 (20%), other (POPS/plazas/transit) 26 (2%)
  - Accessibility: 619 fully accessible (58%), 218 not accessible (20%), 53 partial (5%),
    174 limited/unreported (16%)
  - Council district range: average 19/district; high = District 11 (35); low =
    District 43 (4)
  https://council.nyc.gov/data/public-restroom-analysis/ (accessed 2026-09-20; PDF version
  at council.nyc.gov/press/wp-content/uploads/sites/56/2025/12/OID_Restrooms-REPORT_121625-v4.pdf
  returned unreadable/binary to automated fetch — a human should open directly for exact
  page citations)
- **Earlier council reports, for the "quality is also broken" argument**:
  - *"Nature's Call: An Assessment of NYC Park Restrooms"* (OID, 2024) — inspected
    restrooms with chronically low Parks Inspection Program ratings/complaints, by
    council district. https://council.nyc.gov/press/2024/09/05/2689/ (accessed 2026-09-20)
  - *"Good to Go?"* (2025) — surveyed 337 bathrooms at 172 sites; **40% lacked basic
    amenities** (soap, toilet paper, trash, hand-drying). Cited in search summary of
    council reporting (accessed 2026-09-20) — **recommend verifying directly on
    council.nyc.gov before quoting the 40% figure in a deck**, as this was found via
    aggregated search summary, not a direct document open.

**Framing value**: the city has a *legally binding, dated, numeric target* (2,120 by
2035) against a *published current baseline* (973 operational, 49.42% five-minute-walk
coverage) — this is strong grounds to frame the project's output as "which of the
remaining ~1,150 restrooms needed to hit the 2035 target should be sited first," rather
than inventing an arbitrary target ourselves.

## 3. Benefit side — thin, but not empty

This is genuinely the weakest-evidenced part of the brief. No rigorous US CBA of public
toilet provision was found. What exists:

- **UK, cost side only (not WTP)**: a district-level UK study found each visit to a
  local public toilet cost the taxpayer **£8.69**; a broader review found conveniences
  across West Oxfordshire require **~£270,000/year subsidy** even after user charges.
  Found via search snippet only — **source publication/author not independently
  verified in this pass; treat as an unverified but plausible order-of-magnitude, not a
  citable hard number without further tracing.**
- **IFS ("Institute for Fiscal Studies") publication "The costs and benefits of investing
  in a toilet"** exists (peer-reviewed-adjacent UK think tank, high tier) but the page
  returned 403 to automated fetch; **could not verify its actual figures in this pass —
  a human should open https://ifs.org.uk/publications/costs-and-benefits-investing-toilet
  directly.** Flagging its existence because IFS is a credible enough source to be worth
  the manual follow-up.
- **No dollar-value WTP or formal CBA study for US public toilets was found.** This
  confirms the brief's own expectation that the benefit side is hard — **recommend the
  model use a cost-avoidance/proxy approach (311 complaints, public urination summons,
  sanitation costs) rather than claim a formal welfare number that doesn't exist.**

### Health/medical-condition population share (best-evidenced sub-finding here)
- **Crohn's & Colitis Foundation national survey (2024)**: **84% of people with
  ulcerative colitis** and **53% of all IBD respondents** report difficulty accessing a
  public restroom in the past 7 days; ~1 in 6 IBD patients experience incontinence;
  fecal incontinence prevalence in IBD populations generally cited at **56–63%** across
  studies (advocacy-report tier, citing clinical literature).
  https://www.crohnscolitisfoundation.org/new-national-survey-shows-lack-of-public-restrooms-widespread-public-health-issue
  (accessed 2026-09-20); corroborating PR Newswire release, 2024.
- **General population**: nearly 20% of US adults report ever having had a public bowel
  or urinary accident; ~8% in the past 12 months. Fecal incontinence in the general adult
  population is separately estimated at 2–10% (clinical literature range), corroborating
  the advocacy figure's order of magnitude.
- **"18 US states have restroom-access laws"** (so-called "Ally's Law" statutes requiring
  businesses to let IBD/medical-need patrons use employee restrooms) — cited in the same
  advocacy source; **not independently verified against a legal database in this pass.**
- **"8 public toilets per 100,000 US residents"** — a frequently-repeated national figure
  appearing in advocacy/journalism pieces; **could not trace this to a primary source or
  methodology in this pass. Flag explicitly: this number is widely quoted but its
  provenance is unclear — do not present it as more solid than the NYC-specific Comptroller
  figure (93rd of 100 largest cities), which has a named, findable source.**

### Public urination enforcement (weak proxy for the "cost of not providing" argument)
- NYPD issued **10,003 public urination summonses** in the most recent reported year
  (Mayor's Management Report, via AOL/news aggregation) — fine is **$50** if pled by mail
  under NYC Admin Code §16-118 (a misdemeanor charge under Health Code §153.09 carries up
  to $500/1 year). **This gives a headline number but NOT a real enforcement-cost or
  street-cleaning-cost figure** — summons revenue/cost is trivial next to any restroom
  capital cost, so this data point is only useful as a "symptom exists and is measured"
  citation, not as a dollar benefit input to the model. No source quantifying NYC
  sanitation/street-cleaning cost attributable specifically to public urination was found
  — **gap.**

## 4. Method precedent — the strongest finding for "don't invent a new index"

- **Best candidate: Two-Step Floating Catchment Area (2SFCA), specifically the Gaussian-
  weighted variant (G2SFCA), applied directly to public toilets.**
  Peer-reviewed paper: *"Optimization of a Layout for Public Toilets Based on Evaluation
  of Accessibility Through the Gaussian Two-Step Floating Catchment Area Approach,"*
  published in *ISPRS International Journal of Geo-Information* (MDPI), 2025 — case
  study Kunming, China. Method: replaces a fixed search radius with a distance-decay
  (Gaussian) function; evaluates accessibility at walking-time thresholds of 5, 10, 15,
  20, 30 minutes — i.e. directly comparable to the NYC Council's own "5-minute walk"
  metric. https://www.mdpi.com/2220-9964/14/7/242 (accessed 2026-09-20)
  **2SFCA itself is well-established (originally for healthcare access, widely used in
  urban planning since the 2000s) — this paper is the direct restroom application of an
  otherwise generic, citable method. Implementation difficulty: moderate — needs a
  network-distance or walking-time matrix between demand points and restroom locations
  plus a chosen decay function/bandwidth; standard GIS libraries (e.g. R's `sf`/`osrm`
  or Python's `pandana`) support this. This is buildable within the project's existing
  toolchain (sf/leaflet already installed per CONVENTIONS.md) without inventing a new
  weighting scheme from scratch — recommend using 2SFCA/G2SFCA as the accessibility
  layer instead of/alongside the ad hoc RGI-style weighted index, or at minimum citing
  it to validate that a weighted-demand approach has academic precedent.**
- **Second candidate, non-GIS but directly about restrooms and rigor**: *"Developing
  evidence for building sanitation justice: A multi-methods approach to understanding
  public restroom quantity, quality, accessibility, and user experiences,"* PLOS ONE,
  2023 (San Diego County / Pacific Beach case study). Combines (1) a spatial database of
  restrooms built from admin records + Google Earth + public records requests, (2) a
  structured field-assessment tool for restroom quality, (3) researcher-administered
  survey of restroom-reliant users (people experiencing homelessness, n=45 analyzed).
  Key findings: only 7 public restrooms in Pacific Beach; 71.4% lacked hot water; 78% of
  surveyed users reported relieving themselves somewhere not meant for it; 67% cited
  distance as the barrier. **This is the strongest precedent for a mixed quantity+quality
  scoring approach (not just counting toilets) and explicitly states its tools are
  "adaptable" to other cities** — directly citable as methodological cover for treating
  "operational but low-quality" restrooms differently from truly absent ones.
  https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0288525
  (accessed 2026-09-20)
- A broader, non-restroom-specific methodology reference worth having on hand: Katz,
  *"Facilitating Movement: Assessing the Adequacy, Accessibility, and [Equity of Public
  Restrooms]"* (Greater Greater Washington policy paper) — DC-focused, planning-advocacy
  tier rather than peer-reviewed, but useful for plain-language framing of accessibility
  criteria. https://ggwash.org/files/FacilitatingMovementPublicVersion.pdf (accessed
  2026-09-20, not deeply read this pass).

## 5. Comparison cities

- **NYC ranks 93rd of the 100 largest US cities** in Parks comfort stations per capita
  (NYC Comptroller, "Discomfort Stations," see Section 1). This is the single most
  defensible comparison figure — it's a government audit, not a listicle.
- **NYC ≈ 1 restroom per 9,035 residents** citywide across all restroom types (Council
  assessment, Dec 2025 — see Section 2), i.e. roughly **11 restrooms per 100,000**
  residents if evenly distributed (it is not — see district range 4–35 above).
- **London ≈ 1:5,840** (better ratio than NYC) — figure from a search-aggregated source
  including toilets in municipal buildings, malls, hospitals, and retail; **methodology
  not independently verified, treat as indicative not precise.**
- Could **not verify** a clean, sourced Tokyo or Paris per-100,000 figure in this pass
  despite searching. A commercial "Global Restroom Index 2026" (qssupplies.co.uk, a UK
  toilet-supplies retailer — industry/marketing tier, not academic) claims to rank
  cities/countries but the page returned 403 to fetch and its methodology could not be
  checked; **do not cite specific numbers from it without a human opening the page and
  checking methodology first — this is exactly the kind of quotable-but-unverified figure
  the brief warned about.**
