The NYC Restroom Gap

PMBA6093 · Analytics for Managers · Final project proposal

When nature calls, where should New York answer?

New York City has set itself a target of 2,120 public restrooms by 2035 and is legally required to publish a strategy for reaching it. It has no published method for deciding where they go. This is an attempt at one — and what the data says when you actually run it.

The short version

  • The city has committed to a number and owes a plan. Local Law 58 of 2025 does not require New York to build anything — it requires a citywide bathroom strategy, updated every four years, built around a target metric of at least 2,120 public bathrooms by 2035, half publicly owned. Today there are 973 operational and only 8 open 24/7. The strategy is due; the siting method is missing.
  • It is not a money problem. NYC's own capital records put the median restroom project at $1.56M, and the Comptroller ranked the city 93rd of the 100 largest US cities on park comfort stations per capita (2019). It once promised 20 automatic toilets, installed 5, and left 15 in a warehouse for a decade. The failure is siting, not funding.
  • The restrooms are in the wrong kind of place. 824 of 1,066 facilities sit in parks. The demand is on streets — transit hubs, commercial corridors, nightlife districts.
  • We tested that claim three ways. Restroom supply has no association with need once you control for who is actually present; the funding pipeline runs through Parks capital projects, which by definition only reach parks; and a closure analysis that we built, tested and then discarded when it failed its own control — so we claim nothing from it.
  • The cheap fixes are real. Of 29 priority neighbourhoods, ten need nothing built — just longer opening hours. Addressing all of them needs about $11.3M of capital — the price of roughly three traditional comfort stations — plus $0.5M a year to run.

01The decision on the table

This project is not an argument that New York should care about public restrooms. The city has already named a number. The question is where the next ones go — and the city is legally obliged to answer that in writing.

Local Law 58 of 2025 is a planning mandate, not a construction mandate, and the distinction matters. It requires a Deputy Mayor to lead a citywide bathroom strategy and to file a strategic planning report, updated every four years, working toward a stated target metric of at least 2,120 public bathrooms by 2035, at least half publicly owned. Nothing in the law obliges the city to build a single facility.

What it does oblige the city to do is produce a method: a capital strategy, a set of conversion sites, a modular design recommendation and a public map. Against a current 973 operational restrooms, the target implies roughly 1,150 additional facilities — a planning shortfall rather than a legal deficit, and one the city has to explain how it will close.

That is the gap this analysis addresses. The audience is concrete: the Mayor's Office of Operations, which owns the strategy; NYC Parks, which decides what to reopen or extend; DOT Public Realm, which sites units in plazas; and the MTA, which knows which station complexes have weak access.

The precedent that frames everything

Between 2006 and today, NYC's Automatic Public Toilet programme promised 20 units. Five were installed. Fifteen sat in a Queens warehouse for over a decade. The city had bought the hardware and could not decide where to put it.

02What we inherited, and why we rebuilt it

A proposal already existed for this topic. It centred on a composite index — a weighted average of demand and supply measures, with weights chosen by hand (0.35 demand, 0.30 supply gap, 0.15 hours, and so on) and a return-on-investment stage defined as net present value divided by capital cost.

We kept the framing and rebuilt the analysis, for three reasons that are worth stating plainly rather than hiding:

  • Nothing was estimated. A weighted average of rescaled columns produces no coefficient, no standard error, no prediction and no counterfactual. There is no analysis to explain.
  • The weights could not survive a question. "Move demand from 0.35 to 0.30 — does your top ten change?" has no answer if the weights were never derived from anything.
  • The ROI formula breaks mechanically. Extending opening hours has near-zero capital cost, so NPV ÷ CapEx tends to infinity and the decision rule recommends "extend hours" everywhere, regardless of data. We reproduced this bug ourselves on the first run of our own cost model before catching it — see §7.

Two things from that proposal were genuinely good and are retained: the discipline of costing interventions only for a verified shortlist rather than citywide, and the mapping of each decision-maker to the specific decision they can actually make.

The outcome variable, and the trap inside it

Complaints are a proxy for unmet need, and a noisy one. The decisive problem is not general reporting bias — it is a handful of addresses.

Finding

The top 1% of addresses generate 22.6% of all complaints. Ranked on raw counts, East Harlem is the worst place in New York — but 223 of its 384 complaints come from five adjacent addresses on East 122nd Street. The Rockaways rank second, with 286 of 327 from three addresses on Bayport Place.

Raw 311 data does not rank where restrooms are missing. It ranks where one persistent complainant lives next to one nuisance site. De-duplicating to one count per address per month costs 20.8% of records and changes the answer entirely: the top ten becomes Midtown, the Upper West Side, Chelsea, the Village, the Financial District, the Upper East Side, Elmhurst, Jackson Heights, Astoria and Downtown Brooklyn — high-footfall commercial and transit districts, which is face-valid.

Remaining known bias: complaints correlate 0.597 with the share filed online, so app-using neighbourhoods over-report. This biases investment toward Manhattan. We ran the phone-only test rather than promising it; the result is in §06 and it is not clean. Correlation with total 311 volume is only 0.027, so the cruder "some neighbourhoods just complain more" worry is not the binding problem.

04A benchmark before a model

Before building anything, we set a bar: a rule simple enough to be written in one line, which any model has to beat. Ranking neighbourhoods by subway ridership alone predicts complaints with a rank correlation of 0.709.

Our own "clever" one-liner — ridership at stations with no restroom within 400 metres — scored 0.487, materially worse. Filtering to unserved stations destroyed signal rather than adding it. We report this because it is the reason the benchmark exists.

05Three tests of one hypothesis

The hypothesis: New York's restroom stock does not address the demand that generates street urination, because the stock is park-based and the demand is street-based.

Test one — does closing a restroom change anything?

We tried twice to measure this, and report both attempts because the second one fails in an informative way.

The stronger design uses winterization. About 132 NYC restrooms close every winter and reopen in spring; 843 stay open year-round. That is a recurring, dated closure with a built-in control group. We measured summonses for public urination within 250 metres of each facility — narrow enough that a park interior and the street outside are not pooled — with facility and season fixed effects.

Alcohol summonses have nothing to do with restrooms, yet they move almost as much as the outcome. Seasonal restrooms sit where people stop going in winter — parks, beaches, waterfront — so every category of summons falls. This is population seasonality, not a restroom effect, and controlling for parkland only narrows it. We therefore publish no effect estimate. Stating why a design cannot work is a firmer result than a number we would not defend.

The earlier attempt, using park-inspection closure records, failed for a different reason: the underlying field records construction activity rather than closure, and only 151 of 2,057 flagged records actually read "closed". Corrected, it rests on 44 facilities and could not detect anything smaller than about 20%.

Test two — does supply predict need?

Cross-sectionally, neighbourhoods with more restrooms have more complaints (correlation +0.304). Taken at face value, that recommends removing restrooms. It is confounding: the city builds where crowds already are.

In a negative binomial model of complaints on subway ridership, jobs, hotels, restroom count, density, income, poverty, age and borough, with a population offset:

Result

Restroom count has essentially no measured association with complaints — a rate ratio of 1.001 where 1.000 means "no relationship at all" (p = 0.99). We report this as uninformative rather than as a proven zero: existing supply is placed where demand already is, so this coefficient cannot be read as the effect of a restroom. Existing supply neither helps nor hurts once you account for who is present. Subway ridership and jobs predict complaints (a rate ratio of 1.063 means each unit raises the expected count by 6.3%; p<0.001 and p=0.042); share of residents over 65 is associated with fewer complaints (0.964, p=0.002). Hotels (1.124) does not survive clustered errors (p=0.100) and we do not claim it.

Parkland is estimated precisely; need is not. Need's confidence interval runs from 0.92 to 2.57 — the data are consistent with need mattering substantially, or not at all. We cannot claim the city is blind to need. Two further caveats: 76% of these projects began design before 2020, so they could not have responded to 2020–26 complaints, and "need" is itself a model residual, which makes its stated p-value optimistic. And one result actively refutes the easy narrative — poverty doubles the odds of investment. New York is spending in poorer neighbourhoods. This is not a story about class. Midtown–Times Square is the busiest complaint district in Manhattan and has never received a Parks capital restroom project — because Parks capital projects only reach parks, and Midtown has almost none.

Precision matters here: at neighbourhood level the highest de-duplicated count is East Harlem (North) at 99, with Midtown second at 84. Midtown ranks first at community-district level. Both units appear in this analysis and are labelled where used.

Robustness — this one does not fully hold

Because complaints correlate with the share filed online — 0.31 on the series we model — we re-ranked using phone-filed complaints only (36.8% of records). Three of the six above survive: East Flatbush–Rugby (3→1), Brighton Beach (1→2), East Elmhurst (2→5). Three do not: East Harlem (North) falls to 41st, Williamsbridge–Olinville to 52nd, Astoria (E)–Woodside (N) to 123rd. Top-ten overlap is 4 of 10. We therefore present the three channel-robust neighbourhoods as the confident shortlist and flag the remainder as reporting-sensitive.

These are outer-borough, low-supply, largely non-touristy places. Neither the inherited index nor the simple benchmark would have surfaced them. Ranking on raw complaints points at Midtown — which is already the best-served part of the city.

A check we tried to make, and withdrew

We computed the share of New Yorkers within a five-minute walk of an operational restroom — 53.45% — intending to validate it against the city's widely-quoted 49.42%. On tracing that figure, it is not in the Council's published report at all: it appears only on a Council dashboard that discloses no method — no walking speed, no distance threshold, no statement of whether it uses straight-line or street-network distance, and no population denominator (reverse-engineering implies 8,791,055 residents, which matches no published figure). Two coverage numbers computed by unknown different methods cannot be compared, so we withdraw the comparison rather than present a 4-point gap we cannot explain. Our own figure stands on its own stated method.

Public urination is not a late-night phenomenon in this data — but it does peak precisely as the restrooms shut.

The busiest hour for complaints is 18:00 — the hour at which availability falls off a cliff. Of facilities with genuine recorded hours, 86% are open at 15:00, 45% at 18:00, 15% at 21:00 and 7% at 23:00. Roughly a third of complaints fall in a window when most of the network has shut. Only eight facilities operate 24 hours, a figure that matches the Council's own count exactly and was derived independently.

Why only 318 facilities: 525 of 843 carry the identical placeholder string "8am-4pm, Open later seasonally" — Parks Department boilerplate, not a record of when anything opens. Every figure above excludes them. That the pattern holds on the genuinely-recorded subset is what makes it reportable; it also means the city cannot currently answer "what is open at 9pm?" from its own asset register, which is a finding in itself. Complaint timestamps are report times, not incident times.

08Which intervention, and what it costs

Costing runs only on the shortlist — 29 neighbourhoods at an unmet-need ratio of 1.5 or above — never citywide. The cause of the gap differs by place, and so does the fix:

  • Brighton Beach — 38% coverage, one restroom. The facility genuinely is not there. Build.
  • East Harlem (North) — 78% coverage, six restrooms, and still three times the predicted complaints. The buildings exist. The constraint is hours, capacity or condition.

Costs come from NYC's own capital tracker: 191 restroom projects. Median project cost $2,331,000.

A correction worth understanding

128 of the 191 projects state an exact figure; the other 63 are given as bands. Those bands are overwhelmingly the expensive projects — 90% of them are $3M or more, against 28% of the exact-figure projects. An earlier version of this page excluded them and reported a median of $1,559,000. That was the median of the cheap half. Valuing each band at its lower bound — the most conservative choice available — gives $2,331,000, and valuing them at midpoints gives the same figure. The corrected number is 50% higher than the one we first published.

A trap we walked into ourselves

Our first cost model priced "extend opening hours" at zero capital cost — so cost per complaint came out at zero and hours extension mechanically won everywhere. That is precisely the flaw we identified in the inherited proposal. The fix is to annualise every option (modular over 20 years, repairs over 10, at a 3.5% discount rate used throughout) and price hours as incremental staffing.

One input cannot be sourced. The only NYC operating-cost figure available is Bryant Park at $271,000 a year — an attended, premium facility including $14,160 of flowers — which is not a citywide baseline. Staffing is therefore an explicit assumption: $35/hour, four added hours a day.

An earlier draft defended this with a sensitivity test showing the top five are identical at $20, $35, $60 and $100 per hour. Audit showed that test is close to vacuous: hours-extension stops being the cheapest option at $4.86/hour, below every value tested, so the parameter could not move the ranking by construction. The honest statement is narrower: above about $5/hour the ranking is wage-invariant; we did not test below it. The result that genuinely moves the total is the intervention thresholds — across a 27-combination grid the programme cost ranges $0.95M to $1.64M.

Correcting our own earlier draft

A previous version of this page said the programme cost "a third of one comfort station". That compared an annual recurring cost against a one-off capital cost — the same category error we criticise in §02. Annualised properly, one traditional comfort station is roughly $250,000 a year on the same basis and the programme is roughly five times that. The claim above compares capital to capital, which is the comparison that holds. A separate practical point: NYC capital and expense budgets are not fungible, so the $11.3M and the $0.5M a year are two different asks, not one.

09How hard we tried to break the ranking

A ranking built on about fifteen events per neighbourhood invites one question above all others: is any of this real? Four tests.

Can we tell these neighbourhoods apart from noise? No.

This is the most important test on the page, and it is the one that constrains everything else.

The model estimates not just an expected count for each neighbourhood but also how much counts naturally scatter around it. So we simulated 500 datasets from the fitted model itself — a synthetic New York in which no neighbourhood has any unmet need beyond what its transit, employment and demographics explain — and asked what a ranking of that world looks like.

Our shortlist of 29 is smaller than the 33.8 a noise-only world produces. Brighton Beach's 3.82× sits inside the range you get from taking the maximum of 197 random draws. And after correcting for the fact that we are testing 197 neighbourhoods at once, not one is distinguishable from expectation. With roughly 530 complaints a year spread across 197 neighbourhoods — about fifteen each over six years — the data does not contain enough signal to name individual places with confidence.

What this does and does not mean. It does not mean the variation is fake: the model measures real neighbourhood-level scatter well beyond chance, so some places genuinely are worse than their profile predicts. It means we cannot say which ones. A ranking remains the best available ordering if you must allocate something — but it cannot be presented as a list of neighbourhoods proven to be underserved, and we do not present it that way.

An earlier version of this page reported that the top six appear in the top ten in 85–99% of bootstrap resamples. That test was built wrongly — it resampled the model's coefficients while holding each neighbourhood's complaint count fixed, so it could only ever measure coefficient uncertainty and was guaranteed to look stable. It has been replaced by the parametric bootstrap above, which resamples the outcome and gives the opposite answer.

Is it an artifact of how we measured exposure?

A fair objection: dividing by resident population, for a problem driven by people who are not residents, would mechanically favour residential outer-borough areas — which is what the shortlist looks like. We refitted three ways.

Is it just geography?

If unexplained need clustered spatially, the shortlist could be one blob counted several times. Moran's I on the residuals is 0.107 (p = 0.21) — no significant clustering. The model has absorbed the geography.

What it does not survive

Restricting to phone-filed complaints moves three of the six sharply — East Harlem from 4th to 41st, Astoria to 123rd. East Flatbush–Rugby, Brighton Beach and East Elmhurst are the most stable under that test. But given the noise-floor result above, the honest description of all six is the top of the best available ordering, not a set of neighbourhoods shown to be underserved.

One clue as to why a well-supplied area can still show high unmet need: of East Elmhurst's five operational restrooms, one is fully accessible — 20%, against 59% citywide. Supply counted at the door is not supply available to everyone.

10What a restroom would have to achieve

We cannot estimate what a restroom delivers. So we invert the question — the approach the UK Treasury's appraisal guidance recommends precisely when benefits resist measurement.

A median New York restroom project costs $2,331,000, which annualised over twenty years at 3.5% is $164,012 a year. Rather than inventing a benefit figure, we ask how large the benefit would need to be for that to pay for itself — and let the reader judge whether it is plausible.

Read carefully, because the unit matters. These are avoided incidents. What we observe is complaints — roughly 540 a year citywide, and complaints are a small, biased fraction of incidents. So the table says two different things depending on which unit you price:

  • Priced per avoided complaint: one facility would have to prevent 164 complaints a year at $1,000 each — nearly a third of every public-urination complaint in New York City. No single facility does that. On the evidence we can actually observe, the nuisance case does not close.
  • Priced per avoided incident: at 200 uses a day, 164 avoided incidents is 0.22% of uses. That is entirely plausible. But incidents are unobserved, so this is arithmetic, not evidence.

An earlier version of this page conflated the two and concluded the nuisance case "cannot close regardless of how well restrooms work." That was wrong — it priced a benefit in incidents and tested it against a complaint count. The defensible claim is narrower: we cannot demonstrate the nuisance case from observable data, because the only thing we observe is a sparse proxy. Which is exactly why the next framing is the one to use.

So price the same facility as a service instead — what it costs to provide, per person who uses it:

Those per-use figures are capital cost only, and the usage rates are illustrative rather than observed — New York publishes no footfall data for its restrooms. Including operations would raise them materially; the single available operating datapoint, Bryant Park at $271,000 a year, is an attended premium facility and not a usable baseline.

This reframes the tension running through everything above. We spent this analysis trying to establish that restrooms reduce street urination, and the arithmetic shows that even a decisive result could not have justified the spending on nuisance grounds. Complaints tell you where demand goes unmet. They cannot tell you whether to build. That is a siting question — and siting is what the rest of this analysis is about.

The San Francisco study implies roughly 50 avoided reports per facility per year. Applied to New York's ~970 facilities that would be 48,650 avoided reports — ninety times the city's entire annual complaint count. The transfer is arithmetically impossible, which is itself informative: San Francisco was measuring a far higher-incidence phenomenon (open defecation in concentrated areas), and its effect size cannot be moved to New York.

11What we are not claiming

Stated before being asked, because the limitations shape what the recommendation can carry:

  • We did not prove restrooms reduce street urination. Two designs were built and both were abandoned — the first for insufficient power (it could only have detected effects larger than about ±20%), the second because it failed its own placebo test, which yields no evidence in either direction rather than evidence of no effect.
  • Complaints are not need. They are a reported proxy, biased toward neighbourhoods that report online. We de-duplicate the worst distortion and disclose the rest.
  • Closure dates are imprecise. Park inspections occur about 1.9 times a year, so treatment timing is known only to within roughly six months. Facilities also switch status repeatedly rather than once, which standard difference-in-differences handles badly.
  • The restroom inventory is 15 months stale. The catalogue claims November 2025; the underlying rows were last updated 27 June 2025.
  • No benefit valuation exists. There is no rigorous US cost-benefit or willingness-to-pay study for public toilets. We therefore report cost-effectiveness per excess complaint rather than inventing a welfare figure.
  • Cost-effectiveness figures are per six years, not per year. Complaint counts span 2 Jan 2020 to 18 Sep 2026 (6.71 years). Any “cost per complaint” must divide the excess by 6.71 to be annual; an earlier draft did not.
  • Commercial supply is partially measured, and does not change the answer. We mapped 1,886 chain locations (Dunkin, Starbucks, McDonald's and ten others) from city inspection records. Informal supply shows no association with unmet need (IRR 1.075, p = 0.39) and adding it leaves the ranking essentially identical (rank correlation 0.997, top-ten unchanged). The caveat: thirteen chains only, and presence is not permission.

12What we would do on Monday

The analysis ends in a decision, not a ranking. And because we cannot yet measure whether provision works, the first move is designed to find out.

Run a staggered trial of extended hours. Ten of the 29 priority neighbourhoods need no construction — only longer opening. Extend closing time by four hours at those facilities in randomised order over ten months, rather than all at once. Every neighbourhood is treated by the end, so nothing is withheld; the staggering is what makes the effect measurable.

  • Outcome: phone-filed public-urination complaints within 400m — phone-only, because that is the channel-robust series.
  • Cost: about $51,000 a year per facility in staffing, no capital.
  • Decision it produces: the first credible estimate of what a restroom-hour is worth in NYC — the number this entire literature is missing, and the one our own null could not supply.
  • Then spend the $11.3M of capital on the build sites, informed by a measured effect rather than an assumed one.

The three channel-robust priorities — East Flatbush–Rugby, Brighton Beach, East Elmhurst — are where we would start, with East Harlem (North) and Midtown–Times Square as the largest absolute deficits.

13What we are asking

Confirmation that the framing is right before we build the presentation:

  • Is "where should the next 1,150 restrooms should go, and which need building at all" the right question for this audience?
  • Is it acceptable to lead with a null causal result as supporting evidence, rather than burying it?
  • Does the method mix — regression, spatial analysis, logistic classification, a tree comparison reported as a loss, and a proposed staggered trial — sit at the right level?
  • Is it acceptable that our strongest recommendation is an experiment to run rather than a site to build, given we could not measure the effect from observational data?

Decisions already taken that the group has not yet signed off, things dropped from the original proposal, and the questions that need an answer before this goes to the professor.

1. The original proposal's method is gone

The proposal built a Relief Gap Index — demand and supply measures combined with hand-chosen weights (0.35, 0.30, 0.15…). That is not in this analysis. The weights are now estimated from data rather than chosen, because assigned weights cannot answer the obvious question: "move demand from 0.35 to 0.30 — does your top ten change?"

What that costs us: the index was easy to explain and made a tidy slide. The regression is harder to present. What it buys: coefficients with standard errors, out-of-sample validation, and a defence in Q&A.

Kept from the proposal: the two-stage discipline — never cost an intervention outside a verified shortlist — and the mapping of each decision-maker to the decision they control. Both are genuinely good and both survive.

2. The ROI formula was dropped, and it had to be

SROI = NPV ÷ CapEx breaks mechanically: extending opening hours has near-zero capital cost, so the ratio explodes and the rule recommends "extend hours" everywhere regardless of the data. It is replaced by break-even analysis — instead of inventing a benefit figure, we state how large the benefit would have to be and let the reader judge.

The team should know: this means we present no ROI number. Anyone expecting one will be disappointed. The honest position is that no rigorous US valuation of public toilet provision exists — we checked twice.

3. We cannot prove restrooms work

Two causal designs were built and both were abandoned — the second failed a placebo test we set for it in advance. This is the single most important thing to align on, because it shapes the whole presentation.

Two defensible framings, and the group should pick one deliberately rather than drift into it:

  • Lead with it. "Nobody in New York can currently measure whether a public restroom does anything — here is why, and here is the experiment that would settle it." Confident, unusual, and hard to attack.
  • Bury it in limitations. Safer, but a sharp examiner finds it in Q&A and then it looks like something we hoped they would miss.

My recommendation is the first. It is also the reason the final ask is a pilot rather than a construction plan.

4. The shortlist is six names, not twenty-nine

Bootstrap resampling shows the top six hold in 85–99% of resamples. Below that it decays fast — Hunts Point 51%, Brooklyn Heights 31%. And three of the six are sensitive to who files complaints: restricting to phone-filed reports moves East Harlem from 4th to 41st.

The three that survive every test: East Flatbush–Rugby, Brighton Beach, East Elmhurst. The three that are model-supported but reporting-sensitive: East Harlem (North), Williamsbridge–Olinville, Astoria (East)–Woodside (North).

The group needs to decide whether to present three names with high confidence or six with a stated caveat.

5. Claims we withdrew — do not put these back

  • "49.42% of residents within a five-minute walk." Widely quoted and not in the Council report at all. It comes from a dashboard that publishes no method, so our own coverage figure cannot be compared to it.
  • "NYC ranks 93rd of 100 US cities." True, but it is a 2019 figure counting park bathrooms from a self-reported park-amenity survey. Not a statement about all public restrooms.
  • "Ten neighbourhoods just need longer hours." Survives as a cost argument only. Two-thirds of the hours records are Parks Department placeholder text, and facilities with genuine hours give a wrong-signed result.

6. The open questions for Tuesday

  1. Who presents what? Twelve minutes, six to eight people. The analysis is currently single-threaded through one person, and that is a delivery risk, not an analysis risk.
  2. What gets cut? Thirteen sections and six models will not fit. The danger is that compressing for time cuts the caveats — which is where the credibility lives.
  3. Do we send this to the professor before or after the deck? The framing is settled enough to ask now.
  4. Does anyone want to defend the original index? A fair hearing is better than a quiet replacement. If someone can answer the weights question, that changes things.

7. Where I could be wrong

Stated plainly so it can be argued with:

  • The parks thesis is inferred, not measured. We never compared park-sited against street-sited restrooms directly — there are too few non-park facilities to test it. The evidence fits, but "the restrooms are in the wrong place" is an interpretation.
  • The whole ranking rests on one thin, biased instrument. About fifteen complaints per neighbourhood over six years. We tested a denser alternative and rejected it for measuring policing instead of need. That leaves us with the weaker measure by choice, not by oversight.
  • A complaint is not a person in need. The complainant and the person who needed a toilet are different people with opposed interests. That is a real limitation of the outcome and we have no way around it.
